What Counts as an Approved Clip?

Use observable criteria for source meaning, quality, rights, disclosure and the complete agreed deliverable.

The direct answer

An approved clip is a submission that follows the brief, uses approved source material, meets platform and quality standards, includes required disclosure, has a working public link or approved file, and passes duplicate and suspicious-traffic review.

01

Approval means meeting the applicable brief

An approved clip is a specific final version that a named reviewer accepts under the current campaign criteria. It is not simply a clip with views, a participant’s favourite draft or a file that looks polished. The source, creative and publication requirements should be defined before editing begins.

Approval can cover file delivery or a published post, depending on the scope. State the difference. A file may pass creative review while publication remains pending. Payment eligibility may require another agreed condition. Keeping those decisions separate makes the record understandable to participants and administrators.

02

Check source rights and meaning first

The cut should use the approved source within its reuse boundary. Compare the surrounding passage to ensure the hook and edit preserve what the speaker meant. Keep qualifications, relevant dates and context when they affect the claim. An attractive cut that reverses the source is not acceptable editorial work.

For a hypothetical explanation, a speaker’s conditional lesson should remain conditional. Removing “in this situation” to create a universal promise can change the meaning. Check music, guest footage and private information too. The source being approved in general does not automatically approve every segment or adaptation.

03

Apply a practical creative rubric

Assess whether the viewer can understand the topic, follow the explanation and receive a clear payoff. Check speech clarity, caption accuracy, framing and readable placement. The final export should be inspected on an ordinary phone-size view, not only inside the editing timeline.

Separate necessary defects from optional preferences. A wrong name, inaudible word or misleading hook needs correction. A reviewer’s preference for a different transition may be a creative suggestion unless the brief required that style. Clear categories make feedback actionable and keep the acceptance process consistent across participants.

04

Review commercial and publication details

Check required credits, disclosure, product claims, offer dates and platform conditions. When publication is included, inspect the actual post’s caption, account, URL and timing. The post should match the approved asset and any destination requirements.

If the account owner changes the caption or cuts the video after approval, review the affected requirements again. A draft approval should not silently cover a materially different post. Record inaccessible, private or removed posts according to the brief, and give a route to correct missing information. These conditions should not be invented after a clip performs.

05

Use examples to calibrate reviewers and editors

Provide one accepted demonstration and explain its strengths. Provide a rejected demonstration with a specific defect and a possible correction. For example, a cut can have excellent pacing but still fail because the captions alter a number or omit the limitation. Another may be accurate but need a clearer opening.

Keep demonstrations visibly labelled and avoid implying they are real paid campaign results. Use calibration to update confusing criteria before the full cohort starts. If reviewers disagree, identify whether the brief is ambiguous or whether one is applying a personal preference as a requirement. Resolve that disagreement at the rubric level, not through inconsistent participant decisions.

06

Record the decision and the next action

Each record should identify the campaign, brief version, asset version, source, reviewer, timestamp, decision and reason. Revision requests should name the defect and retain the original record. Rejection should explain the criterion and the available appeal route.

Do not substitute a reaction icon for all of that context. The decision should be reconstructable later, especially if it affects payment. At close, distinguish approved files, approved posts, metric-eligible work and paid work. Approval is useful because it makes the accepted scope explicit, not because it creates a vague quality badge.

07

Use a rubric the participant can inspect

Define acceptance before work starts. Include source permission, preservation of meaning, required format, caption accuracy, audio, publication text where relevant and the specified handoff. Separate essential requirements from optional style preferences.

A rubric should be specific enough to explain a decision. “Make it better” leaves the editor guessing; “the subtitle attributes this sentence to the wrong speaker” identifies a repair. If requirements change after submission, record the change and apply the actual agreement’s handling of additional work rather than silently rewriting the original criteria.

08

Annotate one review rather than giving a vague verdict

In a fictional review, an interview draft has correct audio and format but removes the sentence that limits the speaker’s recommendation. The reviewer records the source positions and asks for that qualification to be restored. The item stays under revision; its technical correctness does not settle its meaning.

The next review should inspect the repaired version and the surrounding source again. Record the actual version accepted and any conditions. This example shows why a useful acceptance decision includes a reason and an identifiable artifact, not merely a reaction emoji or a general approval message.

09

Distinguish the file from the complete publication package

An accepted export may still need an approved title, caption, disclosure or thumbnail before publication. State which boundary your review covers. If the file changes after acceptance, decide whether the affected checks must be repeated.

Keep the accepted version easy to identify and avoid publishing a similarly named earlier draft. For projects that end at file delivery, do not invent a post link. For projects that include posting, connect the actual publication to the approved package. These distinctions make the decision useful to the next person in the workflow.

10

Give rejected work a reviewable reason

Identify the failed requirement and the evidence supporting it. State whether correction is possible within the agreed scope, who can answer a disputed decision and what happens next. Treat taste disagreements separately from clear source or technical errors.

At campaign close, inspect repeated rejection reasons. Improve instructions or onboarding where the pattern shows confusion. Do not hide rejected items in an accepted total or equate acceptance with payment receipt. A fair review process gives participants a concrete way to improve and owners a defensible completion record.

Questions about What Counts as an Approved Clip

Does a high view count guarantee approval?＋

No. The clip must meet the source, creative, commercial and publication requirements defined in the brief.

Can an approved file still have a pending post status?＋

Yes. File acceptance and required publication are separate steps when the scope includes both.

Can a reviewer reject an edit for personal style preference?＋

Apply the agreed rubric. Required corrections and optional creative suggestions should be distinguished.

What evidence should accompany approval?＋

The exact source and final version, applicable brief, reviewer, decision reason and timestamp, with publication evidence where required.

Should an editor repeat every check after a small revision?＋

Inspect the checks affected by the change and any dependencies it introduces. A text change may alter a claim or disclosure; a timing change may remove context. Record the revised version and make the acceptance boundary clear.

Can style preferences be part of acceptance?＋

Yes, when they are clearly described in the actual brief. Distinguish required style from optional suggestions. If a preference is introduced later, record the change and handle its effect on scope through the agreement.
